# 01 · Investor Conviction

Seven investor lenses, written as each would actually assess FROLIC — not as
flattery. Where a persona would pass, that is stated.

---

## Peak XV Partners (formerly Sequoia India)

**Why they'd invest.** Category creation in a large, structurally growing
market with a proven global playbook. Poppi returned $1.95bn to PepsiCo in
seven years from founding; Olipop reached ~$400m revenue and profitability.
The Indian functional beverage market is already multi-billion dollar and
compounding at ~10–11%. If a category leader emerges in India, it is a
₹3,000 Cr+ outcome, and the ownership math works from seed.

**Why they'd reject.** Peak XV underwrites *velocity*, and Indian beverage
is a distribution-and-capital game, not a software game. They will ask why
this is not simply a slower, lower-margin version of a US story — and note
that the 40% GST slab caps achievable margin relative to the US comparables
they are anchoring on. Pre-revenue, against a funded incumbent, this is a
pass at seed.

**What changes their mind.** 12 months of D2C data showing month-3 repeat
above 35% and a blind taste win against both Misfits and a mainstream cola.

---

## Accel India

**Why they'd invest.** Accel has conviction in D2C brands that start online
and earn retail. The subscription mechanic, flavour quiz and bundle builder
are genuine CRM assets. If FROLIC can demonstrate D2C contribution margin
positive at ₹84/can net realisation, the story becomes "brand with owned
demand, retail as margin expansion" rather than "another FMCG grind".

**Why they'd reject.** Indian D2C beverage has poor unit economics at low
AOV. ₹4.20/can last-mile on a 12-can order is survivable; on a 6-can order
it is not. They will interrogate whether the ₹999 free-shipping threshold
actually drives basket size or just erodes margin.

**What changes their mind.** Cohort data showing subscription share above
25% of orders by month 9, with sub-cohort retention above 60% at cycle 3.

---

## Fireside Ventures

**The most likely lead.** Fireside is India's most category-relevant
consumer fund and has backed brands at exactly this stage.

**Why they'd invest.** The Indian-flavour wedge is precisely the thesis
Fireside understands — they have seen that "Western format, Indian taste"
travels further in India than "Western format, Western taste". Ten flavours
built from nimbu, jamun, kokum, aam panna and jeera is a real position, not
a skin. They will also value that the founding thesis is taste-first with
fibre as the enabler, not the reverse.

**Why they'd reject.** Ten SKUs at launch is an inventory and working
capital disaster for a pre-revenue brand. They will push hard to launch with
**three**. They will also want to know why 4 g added sugar when the funded
incumbent is at zero.

**What changes their mind.** Cut to 3 SKUs, show shelf velocity of 4+ units
per store per week in 50 stores, and answer the sugar question with a blind
panel.

---

## DSG Consumer Partners

**Why they'd invest.** DSG underwrites gross margin and repeat above all
else. A 55.7% gross margin in Year 1 rising to 66.7% by Year 5 — on net
revenue after a 40% tax — is genuinely good for Indian beverage. The model
reaches EBITDA positive in Year 4 on ₹30 Cr of total capital, which is
capital-efficient by consumer standards.

**Why they'd reject.** DSG is sceptical of heavy marketing ratios. Year 1
marketing at 60% of net revenue signals a brand buying its way to trial. They
will ask what happens when paid spend stops, and they will be right to.

**What changes their mind.** Evidence that organic and repeat orders exceed
40% of revenue by month 12 — i.e. that the brand is compounding, not renting
demand.

---

## Coca-Cola Ventures / Coca-Cola India

**Why they'd engage.** Strategic defence. Coca-Cola watched PepsiCo take
Poppi for $1.95bn and has a structural gap in Indian functional. An early
minority position in the eventual Indian category leader is cheap insurance.
Coca-Cola's distribution could take FROLIC to 500,000 outlets in 24 months —
something no amount of venture capital buys.

**Why they'd reject.** Strategics do not lead seed rounds in pre-revenue
brands. They wait until a brand has proven velocity and then either acquire
it or clone it. Coca-Cola can develop a prebiotic line internally for less
than an acquisition would cost.

**What changes their mind.** Proven regional dominance — one city where
FROLIC outsells every other functional soda — which makes cloning slower and
more expensive than buying.

---

## PepsiCo Ventures / PepsiCo India

**Why they'd engage.** PepsiCo already owns the global playbook via Poppi
and has explicit strategic rationale for an India analogue. They understand
the category's unit economics better than any financial investor. A 3.9x
revenue multiple is the number they themselves paid.

**Why they'd reject.** Owning Poppi means they can launch it in India
themselves. The build-versus-buy question cuts hard against FROLIC unless the
Indian-flavour portfolio is something Poppi genuinely cannot replicate — and
PepsiCo has decades of experience localising flavour.

**What changes their mind.** Evidence that Indian-flavour SKUs outsell
Western-flavour SKUs in the same basket by a wide margin, proving the wedge
is real rather than aesthetic.

---

## A generalist growth fund (Series B lens)

**Why they'd invest.** By Series B the question is only whether the machine
compounds: CAC ₹580 with an LTV:CAC above 5×, EBITDA positive, and retail
expansion ahead. At ₹1,050 Cr post on ₹125 Cr of revenue the multiple is 8.4x
trailing — defensible for a category leader.

**Why they'd reject.** In the conservative case, Series B returns **1.13×**.
There is no scenario where late-stage money in this business is protected by
anything other than growth continuing exactly as planned.

**What changes their mind.** Evidence that retail gross margin holds at
scale, and that the brand has pricing power — a successful price increase
absorbed without volume loss.

## Investor Readiness Score

Scored as the business stands **today**: pre-product, pre-revenue, with a
complete brand system and financial model.

| Dimension | Score | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Market opportunity | 8 / 10 | Large, growing, global precedent is unambiguous |
| Category timing | 8 / 10 | Early but no longer speculative — incumbents validate demand |
| Product differentiation | 6 / 10 | Indian-flavour wedge is real; the fibre/sugar spec is not |
| Brand & design | 9 / 10 | Complete, original, genuinely premium; ahead of the category |
| Team | — | Not assessable from this pack. **This is the largest gap.** |
| Traction | 1 / 10 | None. No sales, no taste data, no shelf presence |
| Unit economics | 6 / 10 | Modelled and coherent, entirely unvalidated |
| Financial model quality | 8 / 10 | Assumption-driven, auditable, internally consistent |
| Defensibility | 4 / 10 | Brand and flavour IP only; no supply or distribution lock |
| Capital efficiency | 7 / 10 | ₹30 Cr to EBITDA breakeven is reasonable for the category |

### **Overall: 5.7 / 10 — not yet institutionally fundable**

That is not a failing grade for a pre-revenue brand; it is an accurate one.
The score is dragged down almost entirely by two things, and both are fixable
within nine months:

1. **Traction (1/10).** Nothing substitutes for it.
2. **Team (unscored).** No founder credentials are documented anywhere in
   this pack. For a consumer brand, investors back the operator before the
   idea. If the founding team includes beverage formulation or FMCG
   distribution experience, that is the single highest-leverage addition to
   the deck.

**Score at a realistic ₹10 Cr seed (post 9–12 months of D2C):** 7.5–8 / 10,
assuming repeat and taste-test data land where the model assumes.

**Recommended action:** do not raise ₹10 Cr now. Raise ₹1.5–2.5 Cr from
angels and strategic operators against one production run and a six-month
D2C pilot in two cities. Raise the institutional seed on cohort data.
